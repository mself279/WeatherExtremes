1. Missouri tornadoes (EF3+, 1950–present)

How has the frequency of significant tornadoes — those rated EF3 or stronger — in Missouri changed over time? This case uses NOAA's Storm Events Database, the longest-running consistent record of US tornado activity (1950–present).

Storm Events exploration

Before running smoothing, look at the raw event distribution. The Storm Events page shows annual counts, the magnitude breakdown, and seasonality directly from the filtered events.

KDE rate analysis

Saving the filtered selection as a dataset and running the kernel-rate analysis turns the noisy yearly counts into a smoothed rate curve.

The headline finding is that significant tornadoes in Missouri have become less frequent over the post-war period — from about 1.6 EF3+ per year in the 1950s–70s to about 1.1 per year today, with substantial decade-to-decade variability. The bootstrap band widens at both ends: in the early years that's partly about data completeness and the F-to-EF scale transition in 2007; in the most recent years it's because fewer events to average over.

Why no GEV section: the tornado scale is ordinal (EF0–EF5), not a continuous magnitude. GEV models the distribution of an annual maximum of a continuous variable. For categorical scale data, the rate analysis shown above is the right tool.

Takeaways

  • The historical rate of EF3+ tornadoes in Missouri has declined over the long run, but the trend is noisy enough that recent low years aren't a reliable baseline for future planning.
  • Tornadoes cluster strongly in spring — about 75% of events occur March–May.
  • The confidence band widens at both ends of the record; the most recent decade carries more uncertainty than the long-run averages suggest.

2. St. Louis precipitation (1938–present)

St. Louis Lambert International Airport has continuous daily precipitation records back to 1938. This case asks two questions: how often do heavy precipitation events (over 3 inches in a day) occur, and how does the distribution of the annual maximum daily precipitation look? The latter question is sharpened by the fact that St. Louis recorded an 8.64-inch day in 2022 — a magnitude that dwarfs the prior 84 years of records.

Frequency analysis (KDE rate)

The rate of 3-inch days oscillated through the record — high in the 1940s–50s, a clear dip through the 1960s–70s, and a rising trend from the 1990s onward. The recent decade looks like a return to the higher rates seen mid-century, possibly higher. Bandwidth choice matters here because the underlying variability is large: a 10-year window shows oscillations you might call a cycle; a 25-year window flattens them into a gentle U-shape. The pattern at h = 9 (the MISE pick) is between the two — visible cycles plus a long-run trend.

Probability analysis (GEV on annual maxima)

Confidence intervals and return levels

The 2022 single-day maximum of 8.64" is the dominant feature of this analysis. The previous record over an 84-year period was about 5.6" — 2022 was 55% larger than any prior observation. Under the fitted GEV, the 500-year return level is roughly 8.6" and the 1000-year level is 9.6". That means 2022 was on the order of a 500-year event under this model, with the parametric CI on the 1000-year level extending up to ~16" — large uncertainty because we have only one observation that far out in the tail.

The bucket diagnostic shows the fit is reasonable for the bulk and moderate tail but underpredicts 1–2.5" events. The ξ ≈ 0.12 puts this in the Fréchet (heavy-tail) regime, as expected for precipitation — but the parametric CI on ξ ([−0.06, 0.29]) includes zero, so a Gumbel-tail model (ξ = 0) isn't statistically rejected by the data.

Takeaways

  • The rate of 3+ inch days at STL oscillated through the record, with a clear dip in the 1960s–70s and a rising trend since.
  • The 2022 single-day maximum of 8.64" is a true outlier — 55% larger than the previous 84-year record.
  • Under the fitted GEV, 2022 was about a 500-year event with substantial uncertainty in the deep tail. The fit can't rule out Gumbel (ξ = 0) at 95% confidence.

3. San Diego precipitation (1938–present)

San Diego's precipitation profile is different from St. Louis's — heavy days are rarer, often tied to atmospheric river events and El Niño cycles. This case asks the same two questions as STL: how often do heavy days occur, and what does the distribution of the annual maximum look like.

Frequency analysis (KDE rate)

The 1.5-inch rate has trended upward since the mid-1970s, a signal that holds across bandwidth choices. The MISE picks h = 30 because the underlying signal is fairly smooth and the score is flat for any reasonably-long window. At higher thresholds (2"+, 2.5"+) the data thins out fast and the rate curves become much less informative.

Probability analysis (GEV on annual maxima)

The GEV fit is a strong match for San Diego — the bucket diagnostic aligns well at both the bulk and upper tail, and the parametric and nonparametric bootstrap distributions overlap almost entirely. The negative ξ (≈ −0.105) puts this in the Weibull regime with a finite upper bound, consistent with the physical fact that even atmospheric river events at this station have an envelope. The 2024 record sits within the parametric return-level CI; expected return periods for events of that magnitude land in the 20–100 year range.

Takeaways

  • The rate of 1.5-inch days at San Diego has trended upward since the mid-1970s, consistently across bandwidths.
  • The GEV fits cleanly — observed and expected bucket counts align across the full distribution; both bootstrap methods give nearly identical CIs.
  • The shape parameter is mildly negative, so the distribution has a finite upper bound — useful for engineering decisions where "the worst-case rainfall" needs a finite number.

4. Dallas cold extremes (1940–present)

Dallas-Fort Worth has continuous daily-minimum-temperature records back to 1940. This case asks both the rate question (how often do nights of extreme cold occur) and the magnitude question (what's the distribution of the coldest night of the year). The recent reference point is the February 2021 polar vortex event.

Data source
GHCN-Daily station USW00013960 (Dallas-Fort Worth)
Element
TMIN (daily minimum temperature)
Thresholds
≤15, ≤10, ≤5 °F (KDE); annual minimum (GEV)
Date range
1940–present
Analyses
KDE rate + GEV on annual minima
Reproduce this on the live site →

Frequency analysis (KDE rate)

This is the cleanest signal of the four use cases. Nights at or below 15°F at Dallas have declined from about 1.8 per year in the 1940s to about 0.8 per year today — a 55% reduction. Nights at or below 10°F and 5°F have followed the same pattern with proportionally smaller numbers. The 2021 polar vortex event is visible as a small recent uptick in the ≤5°F curve, but doesn't change the long-run decline. The CI is tight across most of the record.

Probability analysis (GEV on annual minima)

The GEV fit for Dallas annual minimums is solid — both bootstrap methods give nearly identical parameter estimates (μ≈17.58, σ≈5.56, ξ≈−0.185) and the parametric PDF aligns closely with the empirical histogram. The shape parameter is mildly negative, which means the cold tail of the distribution is bounded below — under this model the annual minimum can't be much colder than about −12°F. The 2021 polar vortex's ~2°F minimum sits well within the fitted distribution; the bucket diagnostic charts above were generated with an earlier version of the code and may show apparent misalignment that would be largely resolved on a re-run.

One conceptual subtlety for cold extremes: GEV models annual maxima, so for annual minimums we fit GEV to −X. The return-level curve in our pipeline is then in the "warm end of the cold distribution" direction — "the warmest annual minimum exceeded once every T years" — which is the opposite of what most users of cold-extreme analyses want. For engineering decisions about cold-weather infrastructure, focus on the frequency analysis above (which is directly interpretable) rather than reading the return-level point estimates as "the worst cold event expected in T years."

Takeaways

  • Cold extremes at Dallas have declined consistently across all thresholds — roughly 50–60% fewer days at or below 15°F compared to mid-century rates.
  • The 2021 polar vortex was severe (one of the coldest minimums in the record) but the frequency analysis still shows a long-run decline.
  • The GEV fit on annual minimums is credible — parametric and nonparametric bootstrap distributions agree and the fitted PDF matches the histogram. The return-level interpretation for min-direction fits is unusual though (warm side of the cold distribution), so prefer the frequency analysis for cold-event planning.

5. How to read this page

Each case is meant to demonstrate one way to use the analysis pipeline on real data. The interpretations are about how to read the charts, not statistical claims that should be cited in research without further validation. If you're using these analyses in a publication or policy document, run the analysis yourself with current data and parameters appropriate to your question, and look at the bootstrap confidence intervals rather than the point estimates alone.

The "Reproduce this on the live site" link on each case loads the same filters into the analysis pages, so you can run the same analysis on a fresh dataset, change a threshold or bandwidth, and see how the results shift. That kind of sensitivity exploration is often more informative than any single result.

Methodology references for each step (kernel density estimation, bandwidth selection, bias correction, GEV fitting, bootstrap) are on the Methodology page.
